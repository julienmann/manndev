Core Web Vitals are three measurements Google uses to judge how a page feels to the people using it. They are collected from real visits in the Chrome browser, not from a lab test, which is why they matter: they describe what your customers actually experience.

They are also part of how Google ranks pages. Relevance and good content still come first, but when two pages are similarly useful, the faster and steadier one has an edge. And whatever Google thinks, a slow page loses visitors before they read a word.

The three numbers

LCP: how long until the page looks ready

Largest Contentful Paint measures how long it takes for the biggest thing on screen, usually the hero image or main headline, to appear. It is the closest single number to "how fast did this load?"

Good: 2.5 seconds or less. Poor: more than 4 seconds.

INP: how quickly the page responds

Interaction to Next Paint measures the delay between a visitor doing something (tapping a button, opening a menu, typing in a form) and the page visibly reacting. It replaced an older metric, First Input Delay, in 2024. A poor INP is the feeling of tapping a button and wondering whether it worked.

Good: 200 milliseconds or less. Poor: more than 500 milliseconds.

CLS: whether things jump around

Cumulative Layout Shift measures how much the page moves while it loads. You know it from experience: you go to tap a link and an image or banner loads above it, pushing everything down, and you tap the wrong thing.

Good: 0.1 or less. Poor: more than 0.25. It is a score, not a time.

How Google grades itA page passes when at least 75% of real visits are in the "good" range for all three. That means your site has to be fast on an average phone on a mobile connection, not only on your office computer.

How to check your own site in five minutes

  1. Go to PageSpeed Insights (pagespeed.web.dev) and enter your home page address.
  2. Look at the top section, "Discover what your real users are experiencing". This is field data from real Chrome visitors over the last 28 days, and it is what Google uses. If it says there is not enough data, your site does not get enough traffic to be measured yet, and the lab score below is your best guide.
  3. Check the Mobile tab first. Most local businesses get the majority of their visits from phones, and mobile is where sites usually fail.
  4. Scroll to the Performance score below. This is a lab test run by Lighthouse. It is useful for spotting problems, but it is a simulation, so treat the field data as the truth.
  5. If you have Google Search Console set up, open the Core Web Vitals report. It groups every page on your site into good, needs improvement and poor.

What usually causes a bad score

Slow LCP

  • Oversized images. A photo straight from a camera or phone can be several megabytes. Resized and saved in a modern format like WebP or AVIF, the same image is often a small fraction of that.
  • Slow hosting. If the server takes a second to answer, everything else starts a second late. Static sites on a global CDN start almost instantly.
  • Too much in the way. Fonts, stylesheets and scripts that must load before anything appears, often from plugins or themes that load everything on every page.

Poor INP

  • Heavy JavaScript. Every chat widget, tracking pixel, popup and slider adds code the phone has to run. While it is running, the page cannot respond to taps.
  • Third-party scripts. Ad networks, social embeds and analytics tools are frequent offenders because you do not control their code.

High CLS

  • Images without dimensions. If the browser does not know how big an image will be, it cannot reserve space for it.
  • Banners and popups injected late. Cookie bars, promo strips and embeds that push content down after it has appeared.
  • Web fonts swapping in. When the real font loads and is a different size from the fallback, lines reflow.

What fixes it

Some fixes are within reach of anyone: compress your images before uploading them, remove plugins and widgets you do not use, and question every third-party script. Each one has a cost that your visitors pay.

The bigger wins come from how the site is built. A page that only ships the code it uses, loads its images at the right size, and reserves space for everything before it arrives will pass Core Web Vitals without heroic effort. That is much easier to do from the start than to retrofit onto a heavy theme. If you are weighing that decision, custom website or website builder walks through the trade-offs.

Why it is worth caring about

It is tempting to treat this as a technical scorecard. It is really a customer experience measurement. A page that appears quickly, responds the moment someone taps, and stays still while they read is a page people trust and use. Faster sites are also lighter, which means less data for your visitors and less energy for every page view. Good scores are a side effect of building it properly.
