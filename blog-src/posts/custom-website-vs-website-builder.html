If you run a small business, you have probably been told two opposite things. One camp says a website builder like Wix, Squarespace or Shopify is all anyone needs. The other says anything short of a custom site is unprofessional. Both are selling something.

The honest answer is that it depends on what your website has to do. This guide walks through the real trade-offs so you can decide for yourself.

What a website builder actually is

A website builder is a hosted platform. You pick a theme, drag blocks onto a page, type your content in, and the platform runs everything for you: hosting, security, updates, the checkout if you sell online. You pay a monthly subscription for as long as the site exists.

That is a genuinely good deal for a lot of people. You can be online in a weekend, you never think about servers, and the monthly fee is predictable.

The catch is in the word platform. You are renting space inside someone else's product. Their design system, their code, their limits, their pricing changes.

What a custom website actually is

A custom website is designed and coded for one business. Nothing is picked from a theme gallery. The layout, the type, the way the contact form works and the way the page loads are all decisions made for your audience and your goals.

The code is yours. It can live on any host, be moved at any time, and be changed by any competent developer. There is no platform between you and your own website.

Where builders win

  • Speed to launch. If you need something online by Friday, a builder will get you there.
  • Low upfront cost. You trade a large one-time cost for a smaller monthly one.
  • Do-it-yourself edits. Changing a photo or a price is easy, and you never wait on anyone.
  • Simple needs. A one-page presence with your hours, your phone number and a map does not need to be engineered.

If that list describes you, a builder is a reasonable choice, and you should not let anyone embarrass you out of it.

Where custom wins

You look like yourself, not like a theme

Builder themes are used by thousands of sites. Visitors may not name the theme, but they recognise the pattern: the same hero layout, the same three-column features, the same footer. When your work is your differentiator, a site that looks like everyone else's undersells it.

Speed, which customers and Google both notice

Builders have to ship code for every feature any customer might use, so pages often carry scripts and styles that your site never touches. A hand-built page only includes what it needs. That usually means faster loading, especially on phones, and it shows up directly in Google's Core Web Vitals.

Accessibility you can rely on

Themes vary widely in how well they work with a keyboard or a screen reader, and you often can't fix the parts that fail. On a custom build, accessibility is part of the spec from the first line.

Ownership and no lock-in

If a builder raises its prices, changes its features or closes your account, moving is painful: most platforms do not let you export a working site, only your content. A custom site is a folder of files you own. You can take it anywhere.

It can grow with the business

Booking flows, client portals, quote calculators, integrations with the tools you already use: on a builder these depend on what plugins exist and how well they play together. On a custom site, they are just features to build.

The real cost comparison

The fair comparison is not the builder's monthly fee against a custom site's quote. It is the total cost over the life of the site, plus what each one earns or loses you.

Add up three years of subscription, premium theme and app fees on a builder, and the gap is often smaller than it first looks. Then ask the more important question: how many enquiries is the current site losing you?

Five questions to decide

  1. Is your website how most customers first judge you? If people look you up before they call or buy, the site is doing sales work and should be built like it.
  2. Do you need it to do something specific? Bookings, quotes, a client area, an integration. The more specific, the more custom pays off.
  3. Does speed on a phone matter to your customers? For most local businesses, the answer is yes.
  4. How much do you care about owning it? If losing access to the platform would hurt, ownership is worth paying for.
  5. Will you actually edit it yourself? Be honest. Many builder sites are never touched after launch, which removes the builder's main advantage.

If you answered yes to two or more, a custom site is likely worth it. If you answered no to all five, start with a builder and revisit when the business grows.

The bottom line

A builder is a fine place to start. A custom website is what you build when the website has become part of how the business makes money. Neither is the wrong choice in the right situation. The mistake is staying on the one you have long after it stopped fitting.
