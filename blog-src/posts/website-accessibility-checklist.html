Accessibility means that people can use your website whatever their abilities or circumstances. That includes people who are blind and use a screen reader, people who cannot use a mouse, people with low vision or colour blindness, and people with cognitive or motor differences. It also includes everyone on a cracked phone screen, in bright sunlight, or holding a coffee in one hand.

A site that only works for some people isn't finished. The good news is that most of what makes a site accessible is simply careful building.

What WCAG 2.2 AA means

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the international standard, published by the W3C. Version 2.2 is the current one. Each guideline has three levels of conformance:

  • Level A: the bare minimum. Failing these blocks some people completely.
  • Level AA: the level most laws and policies point to, and the sensible target for any business website.
  • Level AAA: the highest level, often not achievable for every type of content.

When someone says a site meets "WCAG 2.2 AA", they mean it passes every Level A and Level AA requirement in version 2.2.

Why it matters for your business

It is a lot of people. Disability is common, and many more people have temporary or situational limitations. A site they cannot use is a customer you never hear from.

It is increasingly expected by law. Accessibility rules for websites exist in many places, including Ontario's AODA, federal rules for federally regulated organisations in Canada, the European Accessibility Act, and the ADA in the United States, where website lawsuits are common. Which rules apply depends on where you operate and who you serve, so check your own situation. Building to WCAG 2.2 AA puts you in a strong position almost everywhere.

It helps everything else. Clear headings, descriptive links, text alternatives for images and fast, well-structured pages are exactly what search engines read too. Accessibility and SEO overlap heavily.

A twelve-point checklist you can run yourself

None of these need special software. Set aside an hour, open your site, and work down the list.

Keyboard

  1. Put the mouse away and press Tab. Can you reach every link, button and form field, in a sensible order?
  2. Can you always see where you are? Each focused element should have a clear visible outline. If focus disappears, keyboard users are lost.
  3. Can you open and close everything? Menus, popups and cookie banners must work with Enter, Space and Escape, and must never trap you.

Seeing

  1. Check text contrast. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background; large text needs 3:1. Free contrast checkers will tell you in seconds. Pale grey text on white is the most common failure on the web.
  2. Zoom to 200%. Everything should still be readable and usable, with no text cut off or overlapping.
  3. Don't rely on colour alone. A form error shown only by a red border, or a required field marked only in red, is invisible to many people. Add text.

Content

  1. Give every meaningful image alt text that says what it shows or does. Purely decorative images should have empty alt text so screen readers skip them.
  2. Use real headings in order. One main heading per page, then sections and subsections. Screen reader users navigate by headings the way sighted users skim.
  3. Write links that make sense on their own. "Read our pricing guide" is useful. A page full of "click here" is not.

Forms, touch and motion

  1. Label every form field with a visible label, not just placeholder text that disappears when you start typing, and explain errors in words.
  2. Make targets big enough to tap. WCAG 2.2 added a minimum target size: buttons and links should be at least 24 by 24 pixels, or spaced so a thumb can't hit the wrong one.
  3. Respect reduced motion. If your site uses animation, it should calm down when a visitor's device is set to reduce motion. Movement can cause real discomfort for some people.
Going furtherTry your phone's built-in screen reader (VoiceOver on iPhone, TalkBack on Android) on your home page for five minutes. It is the fastest way to understand how your site sounds to someone who can't see it.

What the checklist can't catch

A self-check catches the most common problems, not all of them. Automated tools such as Lighthouse or WAVE find more, but they still miss most issues that need human judgement, like whether alt text is actually helpful or whether a custom widget announces itself properly. A proper audit combines automated scans, keyboard testing and screen reader testing.

Build it in, don't bolt it on

Accessibility is cheapest when it is part of the plan from the first line of code. Retrofitting it onto a theme you don't control is slow and often incomplete, and accessibility overlay widgets that promise a one-line fix do not make a site conform and can get in the way of the assistive technology people already use.

Every site we build ships to WCAG 2.2 AA: keyboard-navigable, screen-reader compatible, contrast-checked and respectful of reduced motion. It also tends to make sites faster, which matters for Core Web Vitals and for search.
